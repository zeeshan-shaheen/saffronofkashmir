What is Mongra Grade Saffron and Why It Matters

Walk into any spice market from Dubai to Delhi and you'll find saffron priced from AED 10 to AED 300 per gram. The reason lies in a single word: grade.

Read full article

Kashmiri saffron is classified into three grades based on which part of the crocus stigma is used. Mongra - the highest - consists entirely of the deep-red stigma tips, separated from the yellow style. Below that is Lachha, which includes the red part plus some yellow style, and Zarda or Gucchi, which includes the entire stigma with the full yellow portion. Each step down in grade means less crocin (the colourant), less picrocrocin (the bitter principle), and less safranal (the aroma compound).

Mongra saffron gives 20-30% more colour per gram than Lachha grade. In a Zafrani Pulao or a pot of Kahwa, the difference is immediately visible - a rich golden hue versus a pale yellow tint. In aroma, Mongra releases a deep, honeyed scent that lingers long after cooking; lower grades often smell grassy or of almost nothing at all.

The labour involved is extreme. Each saffron crocus produces just 3 stigmas. After hand-harvesting the flowers at dawn - the only time they're open - workers separate the three red filaments from the yellow style by hand, one flower at a time. One kilogram of Mongra requires roughly 150,000 flowers and over 100 hours of skilled hand work.

When you buy Mongra grade, you're buying the most concentrated, potent form of one of the world's most ancient spices. At Saffron of Kashmir, we source exclusively Mongra grade, tested to ISO 3632 Category I.

The Saffron Fields of Pampore: A Centuries-Old Legacy

Kashmir grows around 90% of India's saffron. Its heart is the Karewa plateau at Pampore - the "Saffron Town of India", twenty kilometres south of Srinagar - where some of the finest saffron on earth is grown.

Read full article

Saffron cultivation in Kashmir stretches back to at least the 14th century, when Persian traders are believed to have introduced the crocus to the Valley. The Mughal emperors were among the most ardent patrons of Kashmiri saffron - Akbar reportedly ordered saffron planted in royal gardens across the subcontinent, but only the Pampore variety produced the quality he sought. The reason is the soil: the Karewa formation is a rare geological deposit of glacial silt, mineral-rich, with perfect drainage for the moisture-sensitive crocus.

The harvest happens in a dramatic 15-to-20-day window in October and November, when the purple flowers emerge almost overnight. Entire farming families move into the fields before dawn, picking flowers by hand before the sun opens them fully. Delay even an hour, and the petals wilt and the stigmas lose potency - one of the most time-pressured harvests in the world.

By the early 2000s, the industry was in crisis: declining groundwater, erratic snowfall, and competition from cheaper Iranian saffron pushed many farmers to abandon the crop. The area under cultivation fell from 5,700 hectares in the 1990s to under 2,500 by 2010.

Some families stayed. Saffron of Kashmir grows out of three generations of family saffron farming in Pampore. We work the same fields, sell direct, and keep the Mongra grade the Mughals prized above all spices. Every tin of our saffron represents a direct line from these ancient fields to your kitchen.

How to Test Saffron Purity at Home (3 Methods)

A 2019 study found over 50% of commercially available saffron in the Middle East contained adulterants - dyed corn silk, safflower petals, even coloured paper fibres. Here's how to verify yours.

Read full article

Method 1: The Cold Water Soak

Place 3-4 threads in a glass of cold water and watch for 10-15 minutes. Real saffron slowly releases a deep golden-yellow colour while the thread itself stays crimson. Fake (dyed) saffron bleaches almost immediately, turning pale while the water goes a harsh orange-red.

Method 2: The Baking Soda Test

Add a pinch of baking soda to your saffron water. Real saffron turns the water bright golden-yellow. Adulterated saffron often turns it red, green, or murky brown as the alkaline soda reacts with synthetic pigments.

Method 3: The Rub Test

Rub a single thread between your fingers. Genuine saffron stains your fingers golden-yellow and softens without crumbling. Fake threads bleed colour quickly and disintegrate, often leaving an unnaturally bright orange-red dye.

Note: These are consumer tests. Professional testing uses HPLC spectrophotometry to measure crocin concentration and ISO 3632 compliance. All our saffron is tested to ISO 3632 Category I - report available on request.

Using Kashmiri Saffron in Arabic Cuisine

Saffron has long been part of Arab cuisine - from celebratory Machboos to saffron-scented Gahwa and the delicate Umm Ali dessert.

Read full article

Machboos (Kabsa): The Gulf's answer to biryani. Use 8-10 threads of Mongra saffron, bloomed for 20 minutes in 3 tablespoons of warm (not boiling) water, then added with rose water in the final steaming stage. The goal is a subtle golden colour and floral perfume - not an aggressive yellow. Full Machboos recipe here.

Saffron Gahwa: Traditional Arabic cardamom coffee with a pinch of saffron added to the grounds. Use just 3-4 threads per pot - saffron is a supporting note here, adding warmth and a faint bittersweet edge.

Saffron Milk (Haleeb Zafaran): Bloom 8-10 threads in a tablespoon of warm milk, then add to a full warm cup with honey, cardamom, and pistachios - the Arabic equivalent of Kesar Doodh, drunk daily across Emirati households.

The golden rule: Always bloom saffron in warm liquid (water, milk, stock - never oil) before adding it to your dish, and never grind Mongra saffron dry - it destroys the delicate aromatic compounds.

Kashmir Saffron GI No. 635: What It Actually Means

Every seller online says "GI tagged". Almost none of them can name the GI number, and the tag does not belong to any of them.

Read full article

Every seller online says "GI tagged". Almost none of them can name the number, and the tag does not belong to any of them.

Kashmir Saffron holds Geographical Indication No. 635. That is a real registration with a real proprietor, and the proprietor is not a shop. It is the Director of Agriculture, Kashmir.

Who actually holds it

The application came from the Jammu and Kashmir Directorate of Agriculture, working with SKUAST-Kashmir and the Saffron Research Station at Dussu, Pampore.

Read that list again, because it tells you what kind of instrument this is. An agriculture department, an agricultural university and a research station registered it. Not a trade body, not a brand, not an exporters' council. It was registered to protect a region's name, and the people who hold it are the people responsible for the region's crop.

So when a website says "our saffron is GI tagged", read it carefully. The GI belongs to the region and is administered by the state. A seller does not own it. A seller can at most be a registered user of it, which is a different thing and a thing you can ask them to prove.

We are not going to claim authorised-user status here, because we hold no such certificate. Our fields are in Pampore, inside the GI area, and our saffron is tested by IIKSTC.

What a GI legally does

A Geographical Indication lets rightful users stop third parties from using the name for saffron that was not grown in the region, or that was not made to the code of practice attached to the registration.

That is the whole mechanism. It is a right to object.

Two things follow from that, and both matter to a buyer.

The first is that a GI is enforcement, not inspection. It gives someone standing to act against a misuse after it happens. There is no inspector opening tins.

The second is that the code of practice matters as much as the geography. The registration covers saffron grown in the region and made the way the registration describes. Material grown inside the area but handled some other way is not automatically covered by the name.

What a GI does not do

It is worth being blunt about this, because the marketing implies otherwise.

- It does not certify your individual tin. No lot is inspected because a GI exists. - It does not measure quality. That is what ISO 3632 is for, and the two are unrelated systems. - It does not transfer to a brand. A seller inside the region does not acquire the GI by being inside the region. - It does not stop the phrase appearing on a website. It gives rightful users a way to challenge that, which requires somebody to actually bring the challenge.

None of this makes the GI worthless. It makes it narrower than the phrase suggests.

What you can actually check

Three things are verifiable without taking anyone's word.

- Certification from IIKSTC, the India International Kashmir Saffron Trading Centre, at Saffron Park, Dussu, Pampore 192121. They do origin and grade certification. - The e-auction portals, gikashmirsaffron.com and saffroneauctionindia.com, where GI lots are traded. A seller who deals in GI lots can point at that trail. - Lot traceability. Ask which lot your tin came from. A seller who cannot answer is telling you something.

Saffron moves under HSN code 091020, which is worth knowing if you are ever looking at an import or export document. It is not proof of origin by itself, since the code covers saffron generally, but it is the line to look for.

The crocin number in the application

The GI application cites Kashmir saffron crocin at 8.72 percent against Iranian at 6.82 percent.

That is a real figure from the registration document, and it is the strongest single argument for the region.

It is also worth being precise about what it proves. It is a comparison made in support of an application. It describes the regional crop as characterised for that filing. It is not a measurement of the tin you are about to buy, and it does not mean every Kashmiri batch outperforms every Iranian one. Growing conditions, handling and drying all move the number.

Your tin has its own number, and that number comes from a lab report on that batch. We publish ours. Anyone can publish theirs. Most do not.

Why the phrase gets misused

Partly because it sounds like a quality mark and reads like one on a product page. Partly because it is free to type.

There is also a genuine confusion underneath it. Sellers who buy from growers inside the GI area are handling saffron that the GI describes, and it feels reasonable to say so. The gap is between describing the material and claiming a registration you do not hold.

We would rather stay on the correct side of that line and explain where the line is.

What we would ask a seller

If you are deciding where to spend money, these are the questions that separate sellers who can answer from sellers who change the subject.

- What is the GI number, and who is the registered proprietor? - Are you a registered user of the GI? Can you show the certificate? - Which lab tested this batch, and what were the three ISO 3632 values? - What is the lot number on this tin?

None of those questions are rude. They are the ordinary questions you would ask about anything expensive, and a seller who deals with them daily will have the answers ready.

Why we wrote this

The phrase "GI tagged" is doing a lot of work in this market, and most of it is decorative. The registration is real, the number is 635, and the mechanism it provides is narrower than the marketing suggests.

Knowing that puts you in a better position than most buyers. Our saffron comes from our family's fields in Pampore, inside the GI area, and we would rather tell you exactly what that does and does not mean than let a phrase do the work.

How to Read a Saffron Lab Report

"ISO 3632 Category I" on a label tells you the seller passed. It does not tell you by how much. The number does.

Read full article

"ISO 3632 Category I" on a label tells you the seller passed. It does not tell you by how much.

The number does. Here is how to read one.

The three numbers

ISO 3632-1:2011 measures three things by ultraviolet and visible light spectrophotometry, each at its own wavelength, all reported on the E1% scale.

- Crocin, read at 440nm. This governs colouring strength. - Picrocrocin, read at 257nm. This governs taste. - Safranal, read at 330nm. This governs aroma.

Three numbers, three wavelengths, three different things. A report that gives you one number is not a full report.

What the E1% scale means

You will see the values written as bare numbers, and they are not percentages of anything obvious.

E1% is a standard way of expressing how strongly a solution absorbs light at a given wavelength, normalised so that different laboratories testing the same material get comparable figures. What matters for a buyer is that it makes reports comparable. A crocin figure from one lab can be read against a crocin figure from another, because both are on the same scale at the same wavelength.

That comparability is the entire reason to ask for numbers rather than adjectives.

What Category I requires

For Category I, the thresholds are roughly these: crocin at a minimum around 190 for filaments, picrocrocin above 70, safranal between 20 and 50, and moisture at 12 percent maximum.

Now the honest part. Sources quote the Category I crocin floor as both 190 and 200. They are not all wrong. The thresholds differ between filament and powder, and different published summaries pick different rows from the same standard.

We are not going to resolve that for you by picking the number that flatters us. If you see 190 in one place and 200 in another, that is why. What matters more is the actual figure on your report and how far above the floor it sits.

Passing is not a score

This is the point of the whole article.

Category I is a band, not a value. A batch that scrapes in just above the floor and a batch well clear of it both print "Category I" on a label. They are not the same product, they will not behave the same in a pan, and one of them justifies a higher price.

The same applies to safranal, which has a range rather than a floor: between 20 and 50 for Category I. A figure sitting near the bottom of that range is a passing figure and a faint-smelling saffron.

So when a label says Category I, the correct next question is always: what were the numbers?

Why a red batch can still test weak

Colour is the thing people judge by eye, and it is the easiest thing to be wrong about.

Crocin at 440nm is a measurement of colouring strength in solution. A batch can look deeply red in the tin and still return a modest crocin value, because what your eye reads as red is surface appearance rather than extractable colour. It can also be red because something was added to make it red, which is a different problem covered by a different test.

This is the whole reason the standard exists. Your eye is not a spectrophotometer.

Moisture, and how weight gets padded

Moisture maximum is 12 percent, and it is the quiet one.

Saffron is sold by weight. Water weighs something. A batch carrying more moisture than it should gives you fewer threads per gram, and every one of those threads is a little weaker than it looks. Nothing has been added that would show up as an adulterant. You have simply paid saffron prices for water.

Moisture is also the figure most often missing from a summary that a seller quotes at you. Check it is on the report. If it is not, ask why.

What spectrophotometry cannot see

The three values are chemistry. They do not tell you what the material physically is.

Two other methods cover that.

- Microscopy looks at structure. A genuine saffron stigma has a trumpet shape at one end. Safflower, marigold petal and dyed corn silk do not. - HPLC looks for artificial dyes such as Tartrazine, Sudan dyes and Amaranth, which exist to make weak or fake material look strong.

A full picture needs all three approaches. Spectrophotometry for potency, microscopy for structure, HPLC for what should not be there at all. A report covering only the first leaves the other two questions open.

What a report should have on it

Beyond the numbers themselves, a report worth anything carries:

- The name of the laboratory and its accreditation. - The date of the test. - A lot or batch identifier that matches the tin in your hand. - The method used for each value.

The lot number is the one people forget. Without it, a report is a document about some saffron, somewhere, at some point. With it, the report is about your tin.

Our report

Our saffron is independently tested to ISO 3632 by the India International Kashmir Saffron Trading Centre in Pampore, NABL TC-9209, and we send the report on request. You can see the tins it applies to on our products page.

The short version

A label that says Category I is a pass mark with the score rubbed off. Ask for the score. Ask which lab. Ask for the lot number.

Any seller who tests their saffron has these documents sitting in a drawer. The only reason not to show you is not having them.

Why Most "Kashmiri Saffron" Sold Online Is Not From Kashmir

Kashmir grows saffron on about 3,665 hectares. Do the arithmetic on what is being sold as Kashmiri and the numbers stop working.

Read full article

Kashmir grows saffron on about 3,665 hectares. Do the arithmetic on what is being sold as Kashmiri and the numbers stop working.

This is not an accusation against anyone in particular. It is a subtraction problem.

The land

Saffron land in Kashmir has been shrinking. The area fell from 5,707 hectares in the late 1990s to 3,665 hectares as of 2025.

Where it sits now, roughly: Pampore around 3,200 hectares, Budgam around 300, Srinagar around 165, and a small area in Kishtwar. Around 20,000 families depend on the crop.

That is the entire growing base. Every genuine gram of Kashmiri saffron in the world comes off those hectares. Not most of it. All of it.

The yield

Productivity has been reported rising from about 2.5 kg per hectare to somewhere between 4.42 and 5 kg per hectare, following the National Mission on Saffron launched in 2010-11 with around Rs 410 crore.

Multiply the land by the yield and you get a number in tonnes, not hundreds of tonnes. We will not put a single figure on it here, because the production figures published by different official sources do not agree with each other. That is its own problem and we have written about it separately.

What is not in dispute is the order of magnitude. This is a small crop.

Why the supply cannot simply grow

Saffron does not respond to demand the way an annual crop does.

Crocus sativus is sterile. It does not set seed. Every new plant comes from a corm lifted from an existing field, which means expanding the growing area is a slow physical process of dividing and replanting, not a matter of ordering more seed for next season.

On top of that, the harvest window is a few weeks in autumn and every flower is picked and processed by hand. 160 to 180 flowers make one gram.

So a shortage cannot be fixed quickly, and a price rise cannot summon more supply. The constraint is land and corms and hands, and all three move slowly.

Iran

Iran is the largest producer of saffron in the world, by a wide margin.

That is the context for everything else. There is a large, cheap, competent supply of saffron on the market that is not Kashmiri, and there is a much smaller supply that is. The price gap between them is the entire incentive.

What follows

If the Kashmiri crop is small, and the volume sold as Kashmiri is large, then most of what is sold as Kashmiri is not.

Relabelling is not an occasional scandal in this trade. Given the arithmetic, it is the default. Saffron travels well, looks similar in a tin, and carries no accent.

There is also a softer version that is more common than outright substitution: blending. A quantity of genuine Kashmiri material mixed into a larger quantity of something cheaper, sold under the better name. Everything in the tin is saffron. The origin claim is still false for most of the weight.

The practical version for a buyer: a listing offering Kashmiri saffron at a price that only works for bulk saffron from elsewhere is telling you what it is, if you are willing to hear it.

What actually distinguishes a seller

Not the word Kashmir. Not a photograph of a field, which anyone can source. Three things:

- GI certification, and a straight answer about whether they are a registered user rather than just using the phrase. - A lab report with a lot number on it, so the numbers attach to your batch and not to a generic sample. - An origin the seller will name specifically, down to the fields, and will keep naming when asked twice.

The third one is underrated. Vague origin language survives the first question and rarely survives the second.

We grow ours on our family's fields in Pampore. Our tins are on the products page and the lab report comes with any of them on request.

What we are not saying

We are not saying Iranian saffron is bad. It is not. Much of it is excellent and it feeds most of the world.

We are saying that a thing sold as Kashmiri should be Kashmiri, and that the volume being sold under that name cannot be supported by the land that exists. Those are different complaints and only the second one is ours.

We are also not claiming to be able to identify anyone else's saffron from a distance. We cannot. Nobody can, from a product page. That is exactly why the documents matter.

The GI is the other half of this

Kashmir Saffron holds Geographical Indication No. 635, registered to the Director of Agriculture, Kashmir.

A GI exists precisely because of the problem described on this page. It gives rightful users a way to stop third parties using the region's name for saffron that was not grown here or was not made to the code of practice. It is a legal answer to relabelling.

What it is not is an automatic check on every tin. Nobody inspects a shipment because a GI exists somewhere. The mechanism is a right to object, and objecting requires someone to notice and act.

So the GI helps, and it does not remove the need for you to ask questions. Those two things are both true at once.

How to use this

You do not need to become an expert. You need one habit: when a seller says Kashmiri, ask which fields, which lab, which lot.

The answer, or the absence of one, will tell you more than any amount of packaging. A seller who handles these questions every week answers them in a sentence. A seller who has never been asked will change the subject, and that is the information you were after.

Nobody Agrees How Much Saffron Kashmir Produces

Four official-sounding sources give four different production figures for the same years. One says 19.58 tonnes. Another says 2.6.

Read full article

Four official-sounding sources give four different production figures for the same years. One says 19.58 tonnes. Another says 2.6.

We are not going to tell you which is right. We are going to put them next to each other, because seeing them together is the useful part.

The four series

One government series, by year:

- 17.33 MT in 2020-21 - 14.87 MT in 2021-22 - 14.94 MT in 2022-23 - 23.53 MT in 2023-24 - 19.58 MT in 2024-25

Another official figure quoted in the press: around 8 MT in 2010-11, falling to 2.6 MT in 2023-24.

A growers' association reported the 2025 season at 10 to 15 percent of normal.

And a widely repeated figure puts the long-run average at 11 to 12 MT.

Read those again. The first series has 2023-24 at 23.53 MT. The second has the same year at 2.6 MT. That is not a rounding difference. It is a factor of nine.

These cannot all be true

At least some of these numbers are measuring different things, and nothing in how they are published tells you which.

Plausible sources of the gap, none of which we can confirm:

- What counts as production. Flowers harvested, stigmas separated, or finished dried saffron are three different quantities. - Dry weight against wet weight. Saffron loses most of its mass in drying, and a figure that does not say which it is can be off by a large multiple. - Whose survey. A department estimate built from area multiplied by an assumed yield is a different instrument from a count of what growers actually sold. - Which area is counted. The crop sits across Pampore, Budgam, Srinagar and a small area in Kishtwar, and a series covering some of those is not comparable with one covering all.

We are speculating in that list, and we want to be clear that we are. The published figures do not explain themselves.

The direction of travel is not in doubt

One thing survives the disagreement.

The land has fallen from 5,707 hectares in the late 1990s to 3,665 hectares as of 2025. That figure is not seriously contested, and it moves in one direction.

At the same time, reported productivity per hectare has been rising, from about 2.5 kg per hectare to between 4.42 and 5 kg per hectare, following the National Mission on Saffron. Less land, more yield per unit of land. Which of those wins in the total is precisely what the conflicting production figures cannot tell us.

So a claim that production is collapsing and a claim that production is recovering can both find support. That should make you cautious about either.

Growers against departments

The growers' association figure for 2025, at 10 to 15 percent of normal, is the one that sits furthest from the official series.

That is not automatically the truer number. A trade body reporting a bad year has its own reasons to report it loudly, the same way a department has reasons to report a recovery. Both can be honest and still disagree, because they are counting different things at different points in the chain.

What is notable is that nobody reconciles them publicly.

Why a buyer should care

This is the part that is not academic.

If nobody can measure the supply, then nobody can verify a claim about the supply. A seller who tells you their saffron is from a record harvest, or from a failed one that justifies a price rise, is citing a number that four sources disagree about.

It also means the arithmetic argument about how much genuine Kashmiri saffron can possibly exist has a wide error bar on it. The conclusion still holds, because even the highest figure here is small against global demand. But anyone quoting a precise tonnage at you, including us, should be treated as quoting one source out of several.

How to read the next figure you see

When a production number turns up in an article or on a product page, three questions make it useful or expose it.

- Dried finished saffron, or something earlier in the chain? - Which years, and which districts? - Who measured it, and by what method?

Most published figures answer none of the three. That does not make them wrong. It makes them unusable for comparison, which is the only thing anyone wants them for.

Why we are not resolving it

We could pick the series that suits us. A low production figure makes scarcity arguments easier and supports a higher price. A high one makes the crop look like it is recovering.

Both would be a choice dressed up as a fact, and you would have no way to tell which we had done. We would rather publish the disagreement and let it sit there, because a reader who knows the figures conflict is better equipped than a reader who has been handed one of them confidently.

If that makes this page less quotable than a single tidy number, so be it.

What we would want instead

A single published series that states its basis: dried finished saffron, measured at this point, by this method, for this area. It may exist and we have not found it. If you know of one, we would genuinely like to see it.

Until then the honest position is the one on this page. The figures conflict, the conflict is not explained, and anyone presenting one of them as settled fact has picked a source without telling you.

Five Things Sold as Saffron That Are Not Saffron

The water test everyone repeats online passes some adulterated samples. Here is what it actually catches and what it misses.

Read full article

The water test everyone repeats online passes some adulterated samples.

Here is what actually gets sold in place of saffron, and what each home test can and cannot see.

Safflower

Sold as "Mexican saffron", and the most common substitute by volume.

Safflower petals are flat and floppy where saffron stigmas are not. They are also cheap enough that the economics are obvious: the substitution pays for itself many times over at saffron prices.

In water safflower releases colour, which is exactly why it works as a substitute, and it is why colour release on its own proves nothing. A jar of water turning yellow tells you something dissolved. It does not tell you what.

Marigold petals

Cut and dried marigold petal is cheap, available everywhere, and gives a strong yellow.

Same problem as safflower for anyone testing by colour alone. Marigold is often cut into thin strips to imitate thread shape, which handles the visual objection at a glance.

The giveaway is structure rather than behaviour in water, which is the theme of this whole article.

Dyed corn silk

Corn silk is already thread-shaped, which does most of the deception before any dye is involved. Dyed to the right red, it looks convincing in a photograph and reasonable in a tin.

Of the three physical substitutes this is the one that survives a casual look best, because the shape is right to begin with. Under magnification it falls apart immediately, because corn silk has no stigma structure at all.

Artificial dyes

Tartrazine, Sudan dyes and Amaranth are the ones that turn up.

These are used two ways, and the second is the one to worry about.

The first use is colouring a non-saffron material so it passes as saffron. Crude, and usually caught by structure.

The second is colouring weak genuine saffron so it passes as strong. Everything in the tin is saffron. It is simply worth less than it looks, and a dye has closed the gap. Microscopy will not catch this, because the structure is genuine. Only a laboratory looking for the dye itself will.

No home test finds these reliably. HPLC does.

Moisture loading

This one is not adulteration in the usual sense and it still costs you money.

ISO 3632 sets a moisture maximum of 12 percent. Saffron carrying more water than it should weighs more per thread. You get fewer threads for your gram and each one is a little weaker. Nothing foreign has been added. You have bought water at saffron prices.

It is also the easiest to do accidentally. Saffron pulls moisture out of a humid room, so poor storage anywhere in the chain produces the same result as deliberate loading.

The moisture figure on a lab report is what catches it. There is no home version of this test.

The trumpet

The single most useful thing you can do at home costs the price of a cheap magnifier.

A genuine saffron stigma is trumpet-shaped at one end, flaring out. Safflower, marigold petal and corn silk do not have that structure, because they are not stigmas. Under magnification the difference is obvious once you have seen it, and it does not depend on judging a colour or timing a soak.

This is the test worth learning. It is structural, so a dye cannot defeat it, and it takes about ten seconds per sample.

What the water test really tells you

Drop threads in cool water and wait. Genuine saffron releases colour slowly and the thread keeps its red. Many fakes release colour fast and go pale.

That is a real signal and it catches the crude cases. What it misses:

- Safflower and marigold, which also release colour, just differently. - Any dyed material where the dye is doing the work. - Genuine saffron that is simply weak, which passes the water test and fails on crocin. - Moisture loading, which the test cannot see at all.

So run it, but treat a pass as the beginning rather than the end. The test is good at excluding the worst material and poor at confirming the best.

Two habits that beat any single test

Buy from someone who will name the lot, and read the three ISO 3632 numbers on the report for that lot.

Structure, potency and dyes are three separate questions and no single check answers all three. A magnifier settles structure at home. The crocin, picrocrocin and safranal values settle potency. HPLC settles dyes, and only a lab can run it.

A note on price as a signal

Price is not a test, but it is evidence.

160 to 180 flowers make one gram, every one picked and separated by hand. A listing priced where that labour cannot possibly have happened is describing a different product, whatever the photographs show. The adulterants on this page exist because the gap between the real cost and the advertised price has to be filled with something.

That does not mean an expensive listing is genuine. It means a very cheap one has already answered the question.

What actually settles it

For structure, a magnifier. For potency, the crocin, picrocrocin and safranal values on a lab report. For dyes, HPLC.

Between them those three cover every case above. Any one of them alone leaves a gap, and the gap is where the money is.

What Saffron Should Cost in 2026

160 to 180 flowers make one gram, all picked by hand. That fact sets a floor no seller can go under honestly.

Read full article

160 to 180 flowers make one gram, all picked by hand. That fact sets a floor no seller can go under honestly.

Everything about saffron pricing follows from it.

The flower arithmetic

One gram of dried saffron needs 160 to 180 flowers.

Each flower is picked individually, by hand, in a window of a few days when the crop is open. Each one then has its stigmas separated out by hand. There is no machine step anywhere in that sequence, and there is no version of it that scales the way other crops do.

Put the labour in order and it is picking, separating, drying, sorting. Only the drying is not hands-on for its whole duration, and it is the step that decides whether the previous three were worth doing.

Why supply cannot chase price

Crocus sativus is sterile. It does not set seed.

Every plant is propagated from corms, which means expanding a field is a slow, physical, manual business rather than a matter of buying more seed. A grower who wants twice the area cannot get it next season at any price.

That is the cost base. Land, corms, and a very large amount of careful human work compressed into three weeks each autumn. It is also why saffron prices do not behave like prices for crops that can be planted wider in response to demand.

Reported prices vary widely

As of August 2026, genuine Kashmiri Mongra retails at roughly ₹380 to ₹600 per gram in India. Lower grades carrying more yellow style run ₹150 to ₹200. Wholesale rates are reported around ₹3.5 to ₹3.6 lakh per kilogram, with retail reaching higher. Sources disagree on the upper end. Below about ₹150 per gram the arithmetic of hand-picking stops working.

What we can say beyond the numbers is structural: the gap between the cheapest listings claiming Kashmiri origin and the cost implied by the flower arithmetic is large enough that the two cannot describe the same product.

Why the cheapest listing is a different product

If a price only works for bulk saffron produced somewhere with far more land and far lower labour cost, then that is probably what it is.

This is the same arithmetic we wrote about in the piece on how much genuine Kashmiri saffron can possibly exist. Kashmir has about 3,665 hectares, down from 5,707 in the late 1990s. That is the ceiling on the whole supply. A cheap, plentiful, always-in-stock Kashmiri saffron is a contradiction in terms.

There is a middle case too, and it is more common than outright substitution: a little genuine material blended into a lot of cheaper material, priced between the two. Everything in the tin is saffron. Most of the weight is not from where the label says.

What you are paying for at each band

Roughly, moving up in price, you are buying these things in order:

- Actual saffron rather than safflower, marigold or dyed corn silk. - Stigma tips only, rather than the yellow style padding out the weight. - A tested batch, with crocin, picrocrocin and safranal numbers you can read. - A named origin with a lot number attached to it.

Each of those costs money to deliver. Testing costs money. Sorting to tips only costs labour and throws away weight that a lower grade would have sold you. A seller providing all four cannot match a seller providing none, and should not try.

Weight against potency

This is the part most price comparisons miss.

A gram of Category I saffron with a high crocin value does more work than a gram of a weaker grade. You use fewer threads to get the same colour and taste, so the cost per dish is lower than the cost per gram suggests.

Comparing two tins by price per gram alone assumes they are the same material. They are not. The three ISO 3632 numbers are what make the comparison real, which is why we publish ours.

Moisture belongs in this calculation as well. The standard allows up to 12 percent, and a batch at the top of that range is selling you more water and fewer threads than a drier one at the same weight.

The right unit

Price per gram is the unit everyone shops on and it is the wrong one.

Price per serving is closer, because it accounts for potency. Our 2g tin is AED 65 and gives 15 to 20 servings of tea or rice. Work that through and the cost of saffron in a given dish is smaller than the tin price makes people expect.

The tin price is what causes hesitation. The per-plate cost almost never does.

Our prices

Our tins start at AED 65 for 2g, and the current range is on the products page. The larger tins give a better price per gram, which is the only discount that exists here, because the underlying cost does not fall.

We are not going to argue that our price is low. It is not. The flower arithmetic does not allow a low price for this product, and anyone offering one is solving that problem some other way.

Price stability

Saffron prices do not fall the way prices for scalable crops do, and it is worth understanding why before waiting for a sale.

The land is fixed in the short run and shrinking in the long run, from 5,707 hectares in the late 1990s to about 3,665 as of 2025. The plant is sterile and propagated from corms. The harvest is three weeks of hand labour. None of those respond to a price signal within a season.

So a large discount on genuine Kashmiri saffron is not a bargain appearing. It is a question about what is in the tin.

The one question

If you take one thing from this: ask what the crocin value is, and ask for the lot number.

A price without those two numbers is a price for an unknown quantity, and an unknown quantity is always cheaper.

Mongra, Lacha, Zarda, Super Negin: The Grade Names Decoded

Two completely different systems are in use, one traditional and one laboratory. Sellers mix them deliberately.

Read full article

Two completely different systems are in use for grading saffron, one traditional and one laboratory.

Sellers mix them, and the mixture is where the confusion lives.

System one: what part of the stigma

The traditional Kashmiri names describe the physical cut. What part of the flower ended up in the tin.

- Mongra is the deep-red stigma tips only, separated from the yellow style. - Lacha includes the red part plus some of the yellow style still attached. - Zarda, also called Gucchi, is the whole stigma including the full yellow portion.

Each step down includes more yellow style. The style is part of the flower, so this is not adulteration. It is simply less of the part that carries the colour and aroma, and more of the part that does not.

That is why Mongra costs more per gram. You are buying a higher proportion of the working material, and the sorting that produces it throws away weight the seller could otherwise have sold.

Why the tip matters

The red stigma tip is where the compounds sit. Crocin for colour, picrocrocin for taste, safranal for aroma.

Weight made up of yellow style is weight that does not do much in your pan. Buy a gram of Zarda and a gram of Mongra and you have bought two different amounts of working saffron, whatever the scale says.

This is also why cheap saffron often looks like a bargain and cooks like a disappointment. Nothing was faked. The ratio was different.

Ours is Mongra, tips only, and the tins are on the products page.

System two: the laboratory categories

ISO 3632 is a separate axis entirely. It sorts saffron into categories by measured chemistry rather than by which part of the flower was used.

Category I requires, roughly, crocin at a minimum around 190 for filaments, picrocrocin above 70, safranal between 20 and 50, and moisture at 12 percent maximum. Published sources quote the crocin floor as both 190 and 200, because filament and powder thresholds differ.

The key point is that this system does not care what the cut is called. It measures what is there.

The two systems are not interchangeable

A cut name tells you what part of the flower is in the tin. A category tells you how the contents tested.

You can have Mongra that tests as Category I. You can, in principle, have Mongra that tests lower, if it was dried badly or stored badly or is simply from a weaker crop. The cut does not guarantee the chemistry, because drying and storage happen after the sorting.

It works the other way too. A lower cut with a lot of style in it is carrying dead weight that a chemical test on the whole sample will notice.

This is why a serious answer to "how good is this saffron" needs both. The cut, and the three numbers.

Where Negin fits

Negin is an Iranian and Afghan trade grade: whole, thick red stigmas cut with no yellow style, often with the three threads still joined. Super Negin is a further selection of the longest and thickest.

Neither is an ISO category and neither is a Kashmiri grade. Kashmir uses Mongra and Lacha.

A listing saying "Super Negin Kashmiri Mongra" is stacking two regions' vocabularies, which usually describes a look rather than an origin.

"Super Negin Mongra Category I"

You will see strings like that on listings, and it is worth recognising what is happening.

Three grading vocabularies have been stacked into one phrase. Two of them describe roughly the same thing from different traditions, and the third is a laboratory result. Stacking them looks like three separate guarantees. It is closer to one and a half.

It is not necessarily dishonest. It is imprecise in a direction that flatters the product, which is the most common kind of imprecision in this market.

Powder is a separate question

Anything sold as powder should raise the difficulty level of every check in this article.

You cannot see a cut in a powder. You cannot look for the trumpet-shaped stigma structure under magnification, because the structure has been destroyed. The ISO thresholds themselves differ between filament and powder, which is part of why published Category I figures vary.

We sell whole stigmas only, and that is partly for this reason. A thread can be examined. A powder has to be taken on trust or sent to a lab.

Why the systems got mixed

The two vocabularies come from different places and were never designed to sit together.

The cut names are old, local and descriptive. They come from people sorting stigmas by hand and needing a word for what was in each pile. The ISO categories are recent, international and analytical. They come from laboratories needing a comparable measure for trade.

Sellers reach for both because each answers a question the other cannot, and because more grading words look like more assurance. The result is listings where the vocabulary is doing marketing work rather than describing anything.

What to ask instead

Skip the adjectives and ask four things:

- Is this stigma tips only, or does it include the style? - Which laboratory tested it? - What were the crocin, picrocrocin and safranal values? - What is the lot number on this tin?

Those four answers describe the product completely. Any grade name is a shorthand for some part of them, and shorthand is where the room to mislead lives.

How to Store Saffron So It Is Still Worth What You Paid

Saffron does not spoil. It fades. Most of what people call bad saffron is good saffron stored badly.

Read full article

Saffron does not spoil. It fades.

Most of what people call bad saffron is good saffron stored badly, and the fading is not evenly distributed across the three things you paid for.

The four enemies, in order

Light does the most damage, then heat, then air, then moisture.

- Light breaks down the compounds that carry colour and aroma. A clear jar on a shelf is the worst common storage choice. - Heat accelerates everything. Above the stove is convenient and wrong. - Air means slow oxidation and, more immediately, the escape of the volatile compounds that give saffron its smell. - Moisture is the one that does structural damage, and saffron will pull it out of the room if you let it.

An airtight tin, kept dark and cool and closed, deals with all four. That is why saffron is sold in tins rather than clear jars, and it is why the tin is worth keeping rather than decanting into something prettier.

Safranal goes first

Of the three measured compounds, safranal is the volatile one. It is what you smell.

That means aroma degrades before colour does. A tin that has been open on a warm shelf for a year can still throw good colour into rice while smelling of very little. People read that as "it still works", and it does, partly. You are getting the crocin and losing the perfume.

The practical signal: if you open the tin and it smells faint, the safranal has gone even though the crocin may be largely intact. Expect colour and less scent, and expect to be slightly disappointed in a dish where the aroma was the point.

Why moisture is the expensive one

ISO 3632 caps moisture at 12 percent for a reason.

Saffron left in a humid kitchen takes water out of the air. That adds weight you already paid for as saffron, softens the threads, and moves the batch towards the edge of the standard it was tested against. A tin that tested comfortably inside Category I when it left the lab is not guaranteed to still be there after a year above a kettle.

This is the one form of degradation that changes a measurable number rather than just a smell.

Why the fridge is usually wrong

This is the mistake we get asked about most.

A fridge is cool and dark, which sounds ideal. It is also humid, and every time you take the tin out, warm room air meets a cold tin and condensation forms inside it. You have introduced the one enemy that does the most structural harm, repeatedly.

Unless you can guarantee a genuinely airtight container and you are not opening it often, a cool dark cupboard beats a fridge. A freezer has the same condensation problem with more temperature swing per use.

Realistic shelf life

Stored properly, in a closed tin, dark and cool and dry, saffron stays good for a long time. It does not become unsafe. It becomes weaker.

What changes, roughly in order: aroma first, then taste, then colour last. A tin several years old and well kept will still colour a dish. It will smell like a shadow of a fresh one.

Properly stored, saffron keeps 2-3 years without spoiling. It does not go off, it fades. Safranal drops first, so aroma weakens before colour does. For full aroma, use it within 12 months of harvest.

Those are guides, not a use-by date. Storage decides the outcome more than the calendar does.

Buy the size you will use

There is a practical consequence to all of this.

A large tin bought once and opened weekly for three years will spend most of its life degrading. A smaller tin finished in a few months spends most of its life sealed. The price per gram is better on the larger tin, and the saffron you actually cook with may be better from the smaller one.

That is not a sales argument in our favour, since the bigger tins are the better margin per gram for a buyer. It is just true.

What this means for a return

This connects directly to our Returns Policy.

We will replace a tin that arrived damaged, and we will replace an unopened tin returned with the seal intact within 7 days. We cannot replace saffron that has lost its aroma after being stored open, warm, or in the light, because that is degradation after delivery rather than a fault in what we sent.

That is not us being difficult. There is no way to distinguish a weak batch from a well-treated one after the fact without a lab, and the compounds involved leave on their own schedule regardless of who is at fault.

Where people actually keep it

The three places saffron usually ends up are all wrong, and all three are chosen for convenience.

A spice rack beside the hob gets heat every time you cook. A clear jar in a window looks attractive and destroys aroma fastest of anything on this list. A drawer that also holds the kettle or the dishwasher vent gets steam.

The right place is dull: a closed cupboard away from the cooking, at room temperature, with the tin shut. It is not a storage solution anyone photographs.

The short version

Keep it in the tin. Keep the tin closed, dark, cool and dry. Not the fridge, not above the stove, not in a clear jar on the counter.

Do that and the saffron you paid for is the saffron you cook with in two years' time.

How Much Saffron to Actually Use

Most recipes over-specify saffron by a factor of two or more, and over-steeping makes it bitter, not stronger.

Read full article

Most recipes over-specify saffron by a factor of two or more.

Over-steeping does not fix a small amount either. Past a point it turns the result harsh rather than stronger.

Threads per serving

As a working guide for good Mongra grade, per serving:

- Rice for 4-6 people: 20-25 threads - One cup of milk or tea: 8-10 threads - Desserts, per 500ml base: 15-20 threads

A strong Category I lot needs fewer threads than the same weight of Category III. Thread size also varies with the cut, so treat these as a starting point rather than a rule.

If you want the specific quantities we use, they are written into each of our recipes, measured for the grade we sell.

Blooming

Saffron does not give up much if you throw it in dry.

Warm liquid, not boiling. Boiling water drives off the aroma you are trying to extract, and safranal is the compound that leaves first. Warm milk, warm water, warm stock, depending on the dish.

Give it 10 to 20 minutes. The liquid turns deep gold and the threads stay red. Then use the liquid, threads and all, in the dish.

Choose the liquid to match what you are cooking. Milk for kheer and kesar doodh, water for rice, a spoonful of the dish's own stock for a savoury pot. The saffron carries into whatever it was bloomed in, so blooming in something that does not belong in the dish wastes part of it.

Whole threads or ground

Grinding changes the result.

A pinch ground with a little sugar or salt before blooming extracts faster and more completely, which is useful when the dish is short on time. The grinding medium stops the threads simply sticking to the mortar.

Whole threads look better in a finished plate, and in a rice dish the visible red threads are part of the point. Choose per dish rather than by rule.

When to add it

Late, in most cases.

Saffron added at the start of a long braise spends an hour losing aroma to the air. The colour survives that. The smell does not.

For rice, the bloomed liquid goes in at the point where the grain will absorb it rather than boil it away. For a curry or a stew, near the end. For milk-based drinks and desserts, off the heat or at a gentle simmer.

Why more is not better

There is a point where extra saffron stops adding perfume and starts adding a bitter edge. It is not subtle when you cross it.

The same is true of steeping time. Twenty minutes in warm liquid gets you most of what is available. Two hours does not double it, and long steeping in hot liquid loses aroma out of the top of the pan while you wait.

If a dish is not tasting of saffron, the usual cause is that it was added dry, or added too early, not that the amount was too small. Increasing the amount to fix a technique problem is how people end up with an expensive, slightly harsh dinner.

Cost per serving

This is the number worth doing, because it is not what people expect.

Saffron is expensive per gram and cheap per plate. A dish serving four takes a small pinch. Our 2g tin is AED 65 and does 15 to 20 servings of tea or rice.

Work it out per serving and saffron sits in the same range as many everyday spices used generously. The sticker price on the tin is what alarms people. The per-plate cost rarely does, and it is the only one that matters once the tin is in the cupboard.

Grade changes the amount

A gram of Category I with a high crocin value goes further than a gram of a lower grade. That is the whole point of the number.

So a recipe written for unknown saffron will over-specify for good saffron and under-specify for weak. If you switch grade, expect to adjust, and adjust downwards when moving up in quality.

The same applies when you move from a cut with yellow style in it to tips only. You are getting more working material per pinch, so the pinch gets smaller.

Storing what you did not use

Bloomed saffron liquid does not keep well. Make what the dish needs.

The threads themselves go back in the tin, closed, in a dark cupboard. Aroma is the first thing to leave a badly stored tin, which means poor storage quietly raises the amount you need for the same result, and you will not notice it happening.

If a tin that used to work at a small pinch now seems weak, look at where it has been living before increasing the amount.

The short version

Less than the recipe says. Warm liquid, not boiling. Ten to twenty minutes. Use the liquid and the threads. Add it late.

Start at the low end and add next time. It is much easier to add saffron to the next batch than to take a harsh edge out of this one.

Harvest Diary: This Season in Our Fields

Photographs with dates on them, from our own fields, during the three weeks the crop exists.

Read full article

Photographs with dates on them, from our own fields, during the three weeks the crop exists.

This post is written during the bloom and published during it. If you are reading it, the dates on the images are real.

The window

Saffron gives you a few days.

The flowers come up almost overnight in autumn and each one is open for a short time. Miss the morning and the petals begin to go. The whole crop for the year comes off the fields inside a window measured in weeks, not months.

There is no second attempt. A field that is not picked on the right morning is not picked, and that portion of the year is gone. That is why every step of this is done by hand and at speed, and why the labour cost is what it is.

[YOUR PHOTO]

Picking before the sun

Picking starts before dawn.

Sun and heat open the flowers fully and start driving off the volatile compounds, safranal first. A flower picked in the cool dark holds more of what you are growing it for than the same flower picked at midday.

So the fields fill before first light and the work is done in the cold, bent over, one flower at a time. Whole families come out for it, because the window does not wait and there is no machine that does this.

[YOUR PHOTO]

Separating the stigmas

Every flower is opened and the three red stigmas are pulled out by hand.

This is indoor work, done sitting down, and it takes far longer than the picking. It is also where the grade is decided. Mongra means the red tips only, with the yellow style separated off and set aside. Doing that properly is slow, and doing it fast is how style ends up in the tin and a lower grade gets sold as a higher one.

160 to 180 flowers make one gram of dried saffron. That number stops being abstract when the flowers are in front of you in a basket and you are three hours into a pile that will not become very much.

[YOUR PHOTO]

Drying

Drying sets the final quality and it is the easiest step to get wrong.

Under-dried saffron carries too much moisture, which fails the ISO 3632 limit of 12 percent maximum and pads the weight with water. It also will not keep, because moisture is what does structural damage in storage.

Over-dried saffron loses aroma. Safranal is volatile and heat takes it. You can dry a batch to a number that passes on moisture and still have ruined the smell getting there.

There is a narrow correct answer and it is reached by watching rather than by a timer.

[YOUR PHOTO]

What can go wrong

Plenty, and it is worth saying so in a post like this.

Weather at the wrong moment shortens the window. Rain during the bloom damages flowers on the plant. A warm morning costs aroma before the pickers reach the row. A slow separating day means flowers sitting too long before their stigmas come out.

None of that is visible in a finished tin. It is visible in the lab numbers, months later, which is one reason we test rather than trusting a good-looking harvest.

The rest of the year

Three weeks decide the crop, and the other forty-nine are not empty.

Corms are lifted, divided and replanted on a cycle, because Crocus sativus is sterile and every new plant comes from an existing one. Fields are weeded and watered. Drainage matters, because the plateau soil holds the crop only if the water moves through it.

None of that produces anything you can sell. It produces next year's three weeks, which is the only thing that does.

Why it is done this way

Every slow step above has a reason, and the reasons are all the same reason.

Saffron is three compounds sitting in a small piece of plant material: crocin for colour, picrocrocin for taste, safranal for aroma. Every stage of the harvest is either protecting those compounds or losing them. Picking cold protects them. Separating quickly protects them. Drying carefully protects them. Heat, delay and rough handling lose them.

That is the whole craft. There is no clever technique that adds anything. There is only a series of chances to lose what the flower already made, and the work is in not taking them.
